# PART I — ARCHITECTURAL FOUNDATION

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the constitutional requirements governing the architecture and system design of Veyra.

Its purpose is to ensure that Veyra is developed and maintained as a coherent, understandable, secure, maintainable, extensible, interoperable, and dependable system.

It establishes the architectural principles, responsibilities, boundaries, controls, and governance requirements that all Veyra systems and constituent components must respect.

This document is intended to provide a durable architectural foundation without unnecessarily prescribing programming languages, frameworks, vendors, infrastructure providers, or implementation technologies.

It governs architectural intent and obligations rather than prescribing every technical implementation.

## 2. Architectural Authority and Constitutional Supremacy

The architecture of Veyra must remain subordinate to its constitution, mission, scope, core principles, security requirements, and governance structure.

No architectural decision, implementation, technical capability, or operational requirement may override or weaken an established constitutional requirement.

Where architectural decisions conflict with constitutional obligations, constitutional obligations take precedence.

Architecture exists to implement Veyra's purpose and principles, not redefine them.

The architecture must never become an independent source of constitutional authority.

## 3. Fundamental Architectural Philosophy

Veyra must be designed and evolved according to the following principles:

1. Clarity over unnecessary complexity.
2. Modularity over uncontrolled interdependence.
3. Explicit interfaces over hidden dependencies.
4. Verifiable behaviour over assumptions.
5. Controlled extensibility over unrestricted modification.
6. Interoperability over unnecessary isolation.
7. Maintainability over short-term convenience.
8. Resilience over fragile optimisation.
9. Human authority over uncontrolled autonomy.
10. Constitutional consistency over architectural convenience.
11. Explicit trust over implicit trust.
12. Accountable ownership over ambiguous responsibility.
13. Evidence-based assurance over unsupported claims.
14. Sustainable evolution over uncontrolled expansion.

These principles apply throughout the entire system lifecycle.

## 4. Architecture Before Implementation

Significant systems and components must have an understandable architectural purpose before implementation begins.

Architectural proposals must identify, where applicable:

- The problem being addressed.
- The intended purpose and responsibilities.
- The component's boundaries.
- Its dependencies.
- Its expected inputs and outputs.
- Its relationship with existing components.
- Its data ownership.
- Its security and trust implications.
- Its authority and permission requirements.
- Its operational requirements.
- Its failure conditions.
- Its maintenance and evolution requirements.

Implementation must not become a substitute for architectural reasoning.

Technical convenience alone is insufficient justification for introducing significant architectural complexity or changing established system boundaries.

## 5. Architectural Simplicity

Veyra must avoid unnecessary architectural complexity.

Every significant architectural mechanism should have a defensible purpose.

The adoption of additional frameworks, services, abstractions, infrastructure layers, distributed components, or external dependencies must be justified by actual or reasonably anticipated requirements.

Complexity must not be treated as evidence of sophistication or architectural quality.

Where multiple designs satisfy the same requirements, preference should generally be given to the design that is easier to understand, govern, verify, maintain, and evolve.

# PART II — ARCHITECTURAL GOVERNANCE

## 6. Architectural Governance

Veyra must maintain a defined architectural governance process.

Architectural governance must preserve system coherence, enforce architectural standards, manage significant design decisions, and ensure alignment with constitutional obligations.

It must operate within the governance framework established by Document 00.9 — Governance and Human Authority.

Architectural governance must not acquire independent authority to alter constitutional identity, mission, or governing principles.

## 7. Architectural Authority

Veyra must maintain a designated architectural authority responsible for architectural consistency and oversight.

Its responsibilities must include:

- Maintaining architectural standards and reference models.
- Reviewing significant architectural proposals.
- Maintaining system and component boundary definitions.
- Evaluating cross-system dependencies.
- Reviewing significant architectural risks.
- Maintaining architectural documentation.
- Maintaining the architectural decision register.
- Reviewing proposed architectural exceptions.
- Monitoring architectural compliance.
- Coordinating long-term architectural evolution.

The architectural authority must operate under appropriate human governance.

It may establish technical standards within its delegated authority but may not override constitutional requirements.

## 8. Architectural Decision Classification

Architectural decisions must be classified according to their significance and potential impact.

At minimum, the classification must distinguish:

1. Routine implementation decisions.
2. Component-level architectural decisions.
3. Cross-system architectural decisions.
4. Critical infrastructure and trust-boundary decisions.
5. Constitutional-impacting decisions.

Review and approval requirements must be proportionate to the decision's significance.

Decisions affecting foundational security, platform authority, data ownership, autonomous execution, or human oversight require enhanced review.

## 9. Reserved Architectural Decisions

Changes affecting any of the following must receive appropriate human review and approval:

- Constitutional boundaries.
- Foundational platform authority.
- Security enforcement.
- Trust relationships.
- Data ownership and access authority.
- Human oversight.
- Autonomous execution permissions.
- Core infrastructure.
- The Veyra–AION relationship.
- Critical system dependencies.

No automated component may independently approve a change that expands its own authority.

No component may approve an architectural exception beyond the authority explicitly delegated to it.

## 10. Architectural Decision Records

Significant architectural decisions must be recorded through formal Architectural Decision Records (ADRs).

An ADR should identify:

1. Decision title and identifier.
2. Date.
3. Context and problem.
4. Requirements and constraints.
5. Alternatives considered.
6. Decision taken.
7. Reasons for the decision.
8. Consequences and trade-offs.
9. Security implications.
10. Operational implications.
11. Responsible decision-makers.
12. Review or replacement conditions.

Architectural decisions must remain traceable from proposal through review, approval, implementation, and subsequent evaluation.

Superseded decisions must remain identifiable as historical records.

## 11. Architectural Documentation

Significant architectural components and decisions must be documented.

Documentation should include:

- System context.
- Architectural layers.
- Component responsibilities.
- Dependency relationships.
- Data flows.
- Interface contracts.
- Trust boundaries.
- Deployment relationships.
- Operational assumptions.
- Security enforcement points.
- Known limitations.
- Significant architectural decisions.

Veyra must maintain current architectural representations showing its major systems and their relationships.

Documentation must be updated when significant architectural changes occur.

Outdated documentation must be corrected or explicitly marked as obsolete.

# PART III — SYSTEM STRUCTURE AND COMPONENT DESIGN

## 12. System Boundaries

Veyra must maintain explicit boundaries between its major systems, subsystems, services, applications, and external dependencies.

Each component must have a clearly defined responsibility and scope.

Boundaries must establish:

- What a component owns.
- What a component is permitted to access.
- What services it provides.
- What services it consumes.
- What information it may process.
- What authority it possesses.
- What responsibilities remain outside its scope.

Components must not silently assume responsibilities belonging to other components.

## 13. Architectural Layers and System Topology

Veyra must maintain an understandable architectural model that distinguishes its major functional and authority layers.

At minimum, the architecture must account for:

1. **Constitutional and governance foundations:** The governing documents, policies, and human-authority structures that establish Veyra's obligations.
2. **Core platform infrastructure:** Foundational mechanisms responsible for platform integrity, identity, security enforcement, and essential system controls.
3. **Foundational platform services:** Shared services supporting system-wide capabilities.
4. **Application and domain services:** Components implementing specific application functions and domain responsibilities.
5. **Intelligent and orchestration services:** Systems coordinating reasoning, planning, context, and approved intelligent operations.
6. **Extensions and plugins:** Additional capabilities operating through governed extension mechanisms.
7. **External systems and providers:** Independent services, infrastructure, models, and integrations.

These classifications establish architectural responsibilities and trust relationships rather than mandatory deployment arrangements.

## 14. Core Platform Integrity

The Veyra core must retain control over foundational platform governance and enforcement.

This includes the appropriate ownership or enforcement of:

- Platform identity.
- Authentication and authorisation.
- Permission management.
- Trust-boundary enforcement.
- Execution controls.
- Security policy enforcement.
- Critical audit mechanisms.
- Foundational configuration.

Ordinary application components, extensions, plugins, and intelligent agents must not independently modify or bypass these foundational controls.

Changes to core platform responsibilities must follow the applicable architectural and constitutional procedures.

## 15. Layered Authority and Dependency Direction

Architectural layers must not acquire authority merely because they are technically capable of accessing another layer.

Access between layers must be explicitly authorised.

No lower-trust component may grant itself higher-trust privileges through internal interfaces, configuration changes, indirect dependencies, or alternative execution paths.

Dependencies between architectural layers must be intentional and controlled.

Extensions and application-level components must not become unauthorised authorities over foundational platform functions.

Critical governance and security services must not depend on untrusted extensions for their enforcement.

## 16. Modularity

Veyra must use modular architectural structures wherever appropriate.

Each module should represent a coherent responsibility and expose a clearly defined interface.

Modules must be designed to:

- Minimise unnecessary coupling.
- Maintain strong internal cohesion.
- Support independent understanding.
- Permit controlled replacement or extension.
- Limit the consequences of component failure.
- Support independent testing where practical.

Modularity must not be pursued mechanically where it introduces unnecessary complexity.

The objective is meaningful separation of responsibilities.

## 17. Separation of Concerns

Different architectural responsibilities must remain appropriately separated.

Examples include:

- User interaction and underlying business logic.
- Presentation and data management.
- Authentication and authorisation.
- Policy definition and policy enforcement.
- Orchestration and execution.
- Configuration and application logic.
- Monitoring and operational execution.
- AI reasoning and permission enforcement.
- Memory management and authoritative configuration.
- Deployment management and application execution.

Where responsibilities interact, their interaction must occur through explicit and controlled interfaces.

## 18. Component Ownership and Responsibility

Every significant component must have a defined architectural owner or accountable maintainer.

Component documentation must identify:

- Its purpose.
- Its responsibilities.
- Its interfaces.
- Its dependencies.
- Its data ownership.
- Its authority boundaries.
- Its operational expectations.
- Its known limitations.
- Its maintenance responsibilities.

No critical responsibility should exist without an identifiable component or accountable owner.

Ownership must include responsibility for addressing failures, vulnerabilities, compatibility issues, and architectural non-compliance.

# PART IV — INTERFACES AND INTEROPERABILITY

## 19. Interfaces and Contracts

Communication between components must occur through defined interfaces and contracts.

Interfaces must specify, where applicable:

- Accepted inputs.
- Expected outputs.
- Data formats.
- Validation requirements.
- Error conditions.
- Authentication requirements.
- Authorisation requirements.
- Version compatibility.
- Expected behaviour.
- Operational limitations.

Undocumented assumptions must not become critical dependencies.

Changes to established interfaces must be controlled and assessed for compatibility.

## 20. Dependency Management

Architectural dependencies must be intentional, documented, and justified.

Veyra must avoid unnecessary dependency chains and uncontrolled reliance on external components.

Dependencies must be evaluated according to:

- Functional necessity.
- Security implications.
- Reliability.
- Maintenance status.
- Compatibility.
- Licensing.
- Portability.
- Long-term availability.
- Replacement feasibility.

Critical components must not depend on undocumented or unmaintained external services without explicitly assessed and accepted risks.

## 21. Communication Architecture

Communication between internal and external components must be designed deliberately.

Communication mechanisms must account for:

- Reliability.
- Authentication.
- Authorisation.
- Data validation.
- Timeouts.
- Failure handling.
- Retry behaviour.
- Rate limitations.
- Version compatibility.
- Observability.

Communication channels must never be treated as inherently trustworthy.

All communication must respect established security boundaries.

## 22. Extensibility

Veyra must support controlled extension without requiring unnecessary restructuring of its foundational architecture.

Extension mechanisms must be documented and governed.

Extensions must operate within explicitly defined permission and trust boundaries.

Extensibility must not provide an unrestricted path for bypassing constitutional requirements.

The platform must retain the ability to restrict, suspend, replace, or remove extensions without surrendering control of foundational functions.

## 23. Interoperability

Veyra should support interoperability with appropriate external systems through documented and controlled interfaces.

Interoperability must not compromise:

- Security.
- Privacy.
- Data integrity.
- System stability.
- Human authority.
- Constitutional boundaries.

External compatibility must be achieved through deliberate integration mechanisms rather than uncontrolled internal dependencies.

## 24. Portability and Vendor Independence

Veyra should avoid unnecessary dependence on a single vendor, provider, framework, or infrastructure platform.

Where practical, architectural decisions should preserve the ability to migrate or replace critical dependencies.

Vendor-specific technologies may be adopted when justified by measurable benefits.

Such decisions must document their implications for:

- Portability.
- Cost.
- Availability.
- Data ownership.
- Operational control.
- Long-term sustainability.
- Exit and migration strategies.

Vendor independence is a strategic preference, not an absolute prohibition on specialised technology.

# PART V — AION AND INTELLIGENT SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

## 25. Veyra–AION Architectural Separation

Veyra and AION must retain clearly distinguishable architectural responsibilities.

Veyra provides the governed platform environment, foundational infrastructure, security boundaries, permission enforcement, and system-level controls.

AION provides intelligent capabilities within the authority and execution boundaries established by Veyra.

The implementation of AION must not transfer ownership of Veyra's constitutional or foundational platform authority to an AI model, agent, or intelligent service.

The detailed conceptual relationship between Veyra and AION remains governed by Document 00.6.

## 26. Model Independence and Provider Abstraction

AION should be architected to support different model providers and model implementations where technically and legally appropriate.

Model-specific dependencies should be isolated behind defined interfaces wherever practical.

The architecture should permit model replacement, version changes, and provider transitions without requiring the platform to surrender its governance, security, or permission-enforcement responsibilities.

Model-specific limitations and capabilities must be documented.

The unavailability or replacement of a model must not compromise foundational platform authority.

## 27. Separation of Reasoning and Execution

The architecture must distinguish between:

- Model inference.
- Planning.
- Decision support.
- Tool invocation.
- Permission evaluation.
- Human approval.
- Execution.
- State management.
- Memory management.
- Policy enforcement.
- Audit and monitoring.

AI-generated outputs must not automatically become authorised system actions.

The ability to generate a plan, recommendation, instruction, or tool request does not constitute permission to execute it.

Authorisation must be determined through independently governed platform controls.

## 28. Execution Mediation and Tool Authority

Consequential actions requested by intelligent components must pass through an appropriately governed execution mechanism.

That mechanism must evaluate, where applicable:

- Identity of the requesting component.
- Requested capability.
- Applicable permissions.
- Resource boundaries.
- Required human approvals.
- Policy restrictions.
- Execution context.
- Data sensitivity.
- Audit requirements.

A model or agent must not bypass this mechanism through alternative execution paths.

Tool access must be explicitly granted, appropriately scoped, and revocable.

## 29. Model Substitution, Failure, and Degraded Operation

The architecture must account for:

- Model unavailability.
- Provider changes.
- Degraded inference quality.
- Incompatible model versions.
- Unexpected model behaviour.
- External service failures.
- Model capability changes.

Where an intelligent component becomes unavailable or unreliable, Veyra must retain control of its own security and governance functions.

Critical platform controls must not rely exclusively on the continued availability or correct behaviour of an AI model.

## 30. Context and Instruction Boundaries

Information supplied to intelligent components must be distinguished according to its source, authority, and trust level.

External content, retrieved documents, user-supplied material, and tool outputs must not automatically acquire the authority of platform policy or governing instructions.

The architecture must support appropriate separation between trusted instructions, application context, retrieved information, and untrusted external content.

Context must be validated and appropriately scoped before it is used in consequential operations.

## 31. AI Observability and Accountability

The architecture must support appropriate records of consequential AI-assisted operations.

Where applicable, these records should identify:

- The requesting component.
- The relevant model or service.
- The applicable policy decisions.
- Tool interactions.
- Approval events.
- Execution outcomes.
- Relevant errors and failures.

Observability must remain consistent with privacy, data minimisation, and security requirements.

The system must not imply that an AI-generated result has been independently verified when it has not.

# PART VI — EXTENSION AND PLUGIN GOVERNANCE

## 32. Extension Registration and Identification

Every registered extension must have a unique and traceable identity.

Registration information must identify:

- Name and unique identifier.
- Publisher or responsible maintainer.
- Version.
- Declared purpose.
- Required capabilities.
- Required permissions.
- External dependencies.
- Data access requirements.
- Compatibility requirements.
- Trust classification.

No extension may become a trusted platform component merely because it has been installed or is technically available.

## 33. Capability Declaration and Permission Management

Extensions must declare the capabilities they require before receiving access to protected platform resources.

Permissions must be limited to the minimum capabilities necessary for their approved purpose.

Undeclared capabilities must not be assumed to be authorised.

Extensions must not automatically inherit the permissions, credentials, or data access of the components that invoke them.

Permission changes must be subject to appropriate review and authorisation.

## 34. Extension Validation and Admission

Extensions must undergo proportionate validation before receiving access to protected capabilities.

Validation should consider:

- Identity and provenance.
- Integrity.
- Declared permissions.
- Dependencies.
- Security implications.
- Compatibility.
- Data access.
- Operational behaviour.
- Trust classification.

The level of review must reflect the sensitivity and potential impact of the requested capabilities.

## 35. Extension Isolation and Failure Containment

Extensions must be isolated from foundational platform responsibilities and from unrelated extensions wherever practical.

The failure or compromise of an extension must not automatically compromise the foundational platform.

Veyra must retain the ability to restrict, isolate, suspend, or remove an extension without surrendering control over core platform functions.

## 36. Extension Lifecycle and Revocation

The extension lifecycle must include controlled procedures for:

- Registration.
- Evaluation.
- Approval.
- Installation.
- Configuration.
- Updating.
- Suspension.
- Revocation.
- Removal.

Revocation must prevent further authorised use of the extension's protected capabilities.

The platform must account for extension-owned data, credentials, dependencies, and active operations when an extension is suspended or removed.

## 37. Extension Compatibility

Extensions must declare supported platform and interface versions.

Incompatible or unsupported extensions must not silently operate with elevated permissions or bypass compatibility controls.

Changes to extension capabilities must be subject to appropriate validation.

# PART VII — DATA, STATE, AND MEMORY ARCHITECTURE

## 38. Data Architecture and Ownership

Data must have clearly defined ownership and lifecycle responsibilities.

The architecture must distinguish between:

- Authoritative data.
- Derived data.
- Temporary data.
- Cached data.
- Configuration data.
- Operational records.
- Audit records.
- External data.
- User and tenant data.
- Intelligent-system context and memory.

Each significant data domain must have an identifiable owner.

Data duplication, synchronisation, transformation, and replication must be deliberate and documented.

Data architecture must comply with Document 00.11 — Security and Trust and all applicable privacy obligations.

## 39. State Classification

Veyra must distinguish between different forms of system state according to their purpose, persistence, ownership, sensitivity, and authority.

At minimum, the architecture must account for:

1. Transient execution state.
2. Request and session state.
3. Application state.
4. Persistent user or tenant data.
5. Operational state.
6. Audit and compliance records.
7. Intelligent-system context.
8. Long-term memory.

These categories may be implemented using different storage technologies or combined storage systems, provided their boundaries remain explicit and enforceable.

## 40. State Ownership and Modification Authority

Every significant state domain must have an identifiable owner responsible for its integrity, access rules, lifecycle, and authorised modification.

Components must not modify state outside their assigned authority.

Critical platform state must be protected against unauthorised modification by application services, extensions, agents, or external systems.

## 41. Memory Separation and Authority

Memory used by intelligent systems must be distinguished from authoritative platform configuration, security policy, and governance records.

AI memory must not independently establish or modify:

- Platform permissions.
- Constitutional requirements.
- Authoritative system configuration.
- Security policy.
- Governance decisions.
- Execution authority.

Memory may inform intelligent operations, but it must not independently confer authority.

## 42. Memory Provenance and Integrity

Where appropriate, stored memory and persistent context must preserve information about:

- Origin.
- Creation or acquisition time.
- Responsible component.
- Intended purpose.
- Trust classification.
- Modification history.
- Applicable retention requirements.

The architecture should support distinguishing verified information from inferred, generated, imported, or unverified information.

Memory must not be treated as authoritative merely because it has been stored or repeatedly retrieved.

## 43. Memory Access and Isolation

Access to memory must be governed by explicit identity, permissions, ownership, and purpose.

One user, tenant, agent, or extension must not gain access to another's protected memory through shared execution contexts or indirect retrieval mechanisms.

Context supplied to one task, user, session, or agent must not automatically become available to another.

Context sharing must be explicit, authorised, and appropriately scoped.

## 44. Memory Lifecycle

Memory and persistent context must have defined retention, correction, deletion, and archival rules.

The architecture must support enforcement of applicable deletion and retention requirements across relevant storage and derived-data systems.

Where data is replicated, cached, indexed, or transformed, the architecture must account for the relevant copies and derived representations.

Legal, regulatory, security, and operational retention obligations must be considered.

## 45. Data Integrity and Consistency

Veyra must establish appropriate mechanisms for maintaining data integrity and consistency.

The architecture must define how systems handle:

- Invalid data.
- Conflicting updates.
- Partial operations.
- Failed transactions.
- Duplicate requests.
- Concurrent modifications.
- Synchronisation failures.
- Data corruption.
- Stale information.

Where immediate consistency is not technically appropriate, the consistency model and its limitations must be explicitly documented.

## 46. State Recovery and Integrity Protection

Critical state must have appropriate integrity, backup, recovery, and consistency protections.

The architecture must define how it handles corrupted, stale, incomplete, conflicting, or unavailable state.

Recovery mechanisms must not silently restore unauthorised or obsolete permissions.

Restoration of security-sensitive state must be subject to appropriate verification.

# PART VIII — INFRASTRUCTURE AND DEPLOYMENT ARCHITECTURE

## 47. Deployment Architecture

Veyra must maintain an understandable deployment architecture describing how its systems are built, configured, distributed, operated, and maintained.

Deployment arrangements must preserve architectural boundaries and security responsibilities.

Infrastructure decisions must account for reliability, portability, operational control, resource requirements, and recovery.

## 48. Environment Separation

Veyra must maintain appropriate separation between development, testing, staging, and production environments.

The architecture must prevent ordinary development and testing operations from unintentionally acquiring production authority or accessing production data.

Each environment must have clearly defined:

- Identities.
- Permissions.
- Configurations.
- Secrets.
- Data boundaries.
- Network boundaries.
- Operational responsibilities.

Production privileges must be restricted and granted only through authorised procedures.

## 49. Deployment Governance

Changes to production systems must follow controlled deployment procedures.

These procedures must account for:

- Change identification.
- Validation.
- Approval.
- Artifact integrity.
- Configuration integrity.
- Deployment records.
- Post-deployment verification.
- Failure handling.
- Rollback or recovery.

Deployment must not become an uncontrolled route for introducing unreviewed architectural changes.

## 50. Infrastructure Reproducibility

Infrastructure and deployment configurations should be represented through controlled, reviewable, and reproducible definitions wherever practical.

Untracked manual changes to critical production infrastructure must be avoided and investigated.

Significant infrastructure changes must be documented and traceable.

## 51. Artifact Integrity and Provenance

Software and deployment artifacts must have verifiable provenance and integrity.

Production deployment must not rely on unverified artifacts or uncontrolled modifications.

The architecture should support traceability between approved source changes, build outputs, deployment artifacts, and deployed systems.

## 52. Production Independence

A compromise or failure of a development environment must not automatically compromise production systems.

Environment separation must be supported by appropriate identity, network, credential, and infrastructure controls.

Production access must remain governed independently of ordinary development privileges.

## 53. Deployment Recovery

Critical systems must have documented procedures for:

- Failed releases.
- Rollback.
- Restoration.
- Recovery.
- Configuration correction.
- Service continuity.

Recovery procedures must preserve security boundaries and data integrity.

Where rollback is technically impossible or unsafe, alternative recovery arrangements must be established.

# PART IX — RESOURCE, PERFORMANCE, AND RESILIENCE GOVERNANCE

## 54. Resource Accountability

Veyra must treat compute, memory, storage, network capacity, model usage, and execution time as governed resources.

Significant components must have identifiable resource responsibilities.

## 55. Resource Boundaries

Components and workloads must operate within appropriate resource limits.

Where applicable, limits must address:

- Compute consumption.
- Memory allocation.
- Storage usage.
- Network traffic.
- Execution duration.
- Concurrent operations.
- Model inference consumption.
- External service usage.

## 56. AI Resource Governance

AI workloads must be subject to appropriate limits on inference usage, tool execution, concurrency, context processing, and other relevant resource consumption.

Intelligent components must not independently remove or expand their resource limits.

Resource allocation must remain subject to authorised platform policies.

## 57. Resource Exhaustion Protection

The architecture must account for accidental or malicious resource exhaustion.

Where limits are exceeded, the platform must respond through controlled mechanisms such as rejection, throttling, suspension, or safe degradation.

Resource exhaustion must not cause unauthorised expansion of permissions or uncontrolled execution.

## 58. Resource Visibility and Cost Governance

Significant resource consumption must be observable and attributable to the relevant component, workload, or tenant where technically appropriate.

Architectural decisions involving recurring infrastructure or external service costs should consider:

- Long-term affordability.
- Cost visibility.
- Operational sustainability.
- Capacity requirements.
- External service dependence.

## 59. Scalability and Performance

The architecture must accommodate reasonable growth in system responsibilities, users, workloads, and data volumes.

Scalability decisions must be based on actual or anticipated requirements rather than unnecessary complexity.

Performance requirements should identify measurable expectations where appropriate.

Optimisation must not compromise security, correctness, maintainability, or constitutional compliance.

## 60. Reliability and Fault Isolation

Components must be designed to limit the consequences of failures.

The architecture should support:

- Fault isolation.
- Controlled failure handling.
- Timeouts.
- Appropriate retry mechanisms.
- Resource limits.
- Health monitoring.
- Graceful degradation.
- Recovery procedures.

A failure in one component should not unnecessarily compromise unrelated components.

Critical functions must have documented failure behaviour.

## 61. Graceful Degradation

When a component or dependency becomes unavailable, Veyra should preserve safe and essential functionality wherever possible.

Degraded operation must not silently bypass:

- Security controls.
- Authorisation requirements.
- Required human approvals.
- Data-integrity protections.
- Constitutional obligations.

Where safe operation cannot be maintained, the system must fail in a controlled manner.

The system must communicate significant limitations rather than presenting incomplete or unreliable operations as successful.

# PART X — CONFIGURATION AND OPERATIONAL VISIBILITY

## 62. Configuration Architecture

Configuration must be separated from application logic wherever practical.

Configuration must have:

- Defined ownership.
- Documented meaning.
- Validation requirements.
- Controlled modification procedures.
- Appropriate access restrictions.
- Versioning where necessary.
- Safe default behaviour.

Critical configuration must not be silently modified by components without appropriate authority.

Secrets and sensitive configuration must comply with Document 00.11.

## 63. Observability and Operational Visibility

Veyra must be architected to support meaningful operational visibility.

Where appropriate, components must expose information necessary to understand their behaviour and health.

Observability mechanisms may include:

- Structured logging.
- Metrics.
- Tracing.
- Health checks.
- Performance measurements.
- Dependency status.
- Error reporting.
- Audit records.

Observability must respect privacy, data minimisation, and access-control requirements.

The absence of operational visibility must not be mistaken for evidence of correct operation.

## 64. Operational Accountability

Significant components must have defined operational responsibilities.

The architecture should support identifying:

- Which component performed an operation.
- Which component requested it.
- Which authority permitted it.
- Which resources it consumed.
- Whether it succeeded.
- Whether it failed.
- Whether recovery or intervention was required.

Operational accountability must be supported without collecting unnecessary sensitive information.

# PART XI — TESTING, VERIFICATION, AND ARCHITECTURE ASSURANCE

## 65. Testability and Verification

Architectural decisions must support the ability to verify that systems behave as intended.

Components should be designed to support appropriate forms of testing, including:

- Unit testing.
- Integration testing.
- Contract testing.
- System testing.
- Regression testing.
- Performance testing.
- Resilience testing.
- Security testing.
- Compatibility testing.

Critical architectural assumptions must be verifiable.

Where a requirement cannot be tested directly, an alternative verification method must be documented.

## 66. Architecture Assurance

Veyra must maintain an architecture assurance process that evaluates whether significant systems conform to constitutional and architectural requirements.

Architecture assurance must not be limited to initial design approval.

It must remain applicable throughout the system lifecycle.

## 67. Pre-Implementation Review

Critical systems and high-impact architectural changes must undergo appropriate design review before implementation.

The review must consider:

- Purpose and scope.
- Component boundaries.
- Trust relationships.
- Security and privacy.
- Data ownership.
- Failure behaviour.
- Resource requirements.
- Operational impact.
- Human oversight.
- Constitutional compliance.

The depth of review must be proportionate to the potential impact of the system or change.

## 68. Threat and Risk Assessment

Significant architectural changes must receive appropriate threat and risk assessment.

The assessment must consider the system's exposure, authority, sensitivity, dependencies, and potential consequences of failure.

Security assessment must be consistent with Document 00.11.

## 69. Evidence-Based Assurance

Architectural compliance must be supported by evidence where practical.

Evidence may include:

- Design reviews.
- Interface specifications.
- Test results.
- Security assessments.
- Configuration records.
- Audit records.
- Dependency assessments.
- Operational verification.

Assertions of compliance without appropriate supporting evidence must not be treated as sufficient assurance for critical systems.

## 70. Continuous Assurance

Architecture assurance must continue after initial approval.

Critical systems must be reassessed when significant changes occur or when new vulnerabilities, dependencies, risks, or operational conditions emerge.

The architecture must remain subject to appropriate verification throughout its operational lifecycle.

## 71. Non-Compliance and Remediation

Identified architectural non-compliance must be:

- Documented.
- Assessed for risk.
- Assigned an accountable owner.
- Prioritised appropriately.
- Addressed through an authorised remediation process.
- Verified following remediation.

Non-compliance affecting security, constitutional obligations, data integrity, or human authority must receive appropriate priority.

## 72. Residual Risk and Independent Review

Risks that cannot be eliminated must be explicitly documented.

Risk acceptance must follow the governance and human-authority requirements established by Document 00.9.

No component may accept risk on behalf of an authority it does not possess.

Where potential impact justifies it, critical architectural reviews should be independently validated by reviewers who are not solely responsible for implementing the system under review.

# PART XII — COMPATIBILITY, MIGRATION, AND EVOLUTION

## 73. Versioned Interfaces

Significant interfaces, APIs, schemas, and externally consumed contracts must use controlled versioning where appropriate.

Their compatibility requirements must be documented.

## 74. Backward Compatibility

Changes should preserve backward compatibility where reasonably practical.

Where compatibility cannot be maintained, the change must be identified as breaking and receive appropriate review.

Compatibility decisions must account for security, operational, and maintenance implications.

## 75. Deprecation

Deprecated interfaces and capabilities must have documented replacement guidance and an appropriate transition period, subject to security and operational urgency.

Deprecation must not silently disable critical functionality.

The responsible authority must communicate significant compatibility changes to affected stakeholders.

## 76. Data Migration

Changes affecting persistent data structures must have documented migration strategies.

Migration planning must address:

- Data integrity.
- Validation.
- Compatibility.
- Partial failure.
- Recovery.
- Backup.
- Auditability.
- Downtime or service disruption.

Migration operations must preserve applicable data ownership and access restrictions.

## 77. Migration Authority and Irreversible Changes

Significant migrations must receive review proportionate to their potential impact.

Destructive or irreversible migration operations must receive appropriate human authorisation.

Migration plans must identify whether rollback is technically possible and under what conditions.

Where rollback is impossible, alternative recovery and restoration arrangements must be established before execution.

## 78. Legacy Systems

Legacy components must be assessed for security, compatibility, maintainability, and continued architectural suitability.

Their continued existence must not be treated as automatic justification for preserving insecure or obsolete dependencies.

Legacy dependencies must have identifiable ownership and appropriate remediation or replacement plans where necessary.

## 79. Architectural Evolution

Veyra's architecture must be capable of evolving without losing its constitutional identity.

Architectural evolution must be deliberate, documented, and controlled.

Changes must consider:

- Existing system behaviour.
- Compatibility.
- Data migration.
- Security implications.
- Operational disruption.
- Dependency changes.
- Documentation updates.
- Rollback or recovery requirements.

Architectural evolution must not become an uncontrolled accumulation of exceptions and temporary workarounds.

## 80. Technical Debt

Technical debt must be recognised, documented, and managed.

Temporary compromises must not silently become permanent architectural assumptions.

Significant technical debt should identify:

- Its origin.
- Its impact.
- Its associated risks.
- Its affected components.
- Its remediation requirements.
- Its accountable owner.

Technical debt that threatens security, reliability, constitutional compliance, or data integrity must receive appropriate priority.

## 81. Architectural Integrity and Stewardship

The implementation of Veyra must remain consistent with its approved architectural intent.

Temporary implementation choices must not silently redefine foundational architectural responsibilities.

Components that are no longer required must undergo controlled decommissioning.

Decommissioning must account for:

- Dependent systems.
- Data retention.
- Credential revocation.
- Access removal.
- Operational continuity.
- External integrations.
- Audit and recordkeeping obligations.

Architectural evolution must preserve traceability between original requirements, approved design decisions, implemented systems, and subsequent revisions.

# PART XIII — SECURITY AND TRUST ALIGNMENT

## 82. Architecture and Security

Security must be an architectural property rather than an isolated implementation feature.

Architectural decisions must account for:

- Trust boundaries.
- Identity.
- Access control.
- Data protection.
- Secrets management.
- Attack surfaces.
- Isolation.
- Supply-chain integrity.
- Auditability.
- Secure failure behaviour.

The detailed security obligations remain governed by Document 00.11 — Security and Trust.

Nothing in this document reduces or replaces those requirements.

## 83. Explicit Trust and External Dependencies

Integration with Veyra must not automatically grant an external system, model provider, extension, service, or component elevated trust.

Trust must be explicitly established, limited, monitored, and revocable.

External dependencies must operate within defined permission and data boundaries.

The architecture must account for the possibility that trusted dependencies may become compromised, unavailable, or unsuitable.

## 84. Security Boundary Preservation

Architectural convenience, performance optimisation, or interoperability requirements must not justify silently weakening security boundaries.

Any proposed change affecting established trust relationships or security enforcement must undergo appropriate review.

Security enforcement must remain independently governed from components whose actions it controls.

# PART XIV — EXCEPTIONS AND COMPLIANCE

## 85. Architecture Compliance

Significant systems and components must be evaluated against this document.

Architecture reviews should assess:

- Constitutional alignment.
- Boundary clarity.
- Responsibility ownership.
- Dependency management.
- Security.
- Data integrity.
- Reliability.
- Maintainability.
- Operational visibility.
- Testability.
- Human oversight.
- Resource governance.
- Lifecycle management.

Identified non-compliance must be documented and addressed through appropriate governance procedures.

## 86. Exceptions and Deviations

Architectural exceptions must be justified, documented, and reviewed.

An exception must identify:

- The requirement being deviated from.
- The reason for the deviation.
- The affected systems.
- The associated risks.
- Compensating controls.
- The approving authority.
- The review date.
- Any expiry or remediation conditions.

Exceptions must be limited to the scope for which they were approved.

No architectural exception may override an absolute constitutional prohibition or transfer constitutional authority to an unauthorised component.

## 87. No Implicit Authority Through Technical Capability

No system, subsystem, service, model, agent, plugin, or external dependency may acquire additional authority merely because it has the technical capability to exercise it.

Technical access, successful integration, operational availability, or execution capability must not be interpreted as constitutional permission.

All authority must derive from properly established and governed permissions.

## 88. Relationship With Other Constitutional Documents

This document must be interpreted alongside the other documents in the Veyra Constitution.

In particular:

- Document 00.1 establishes project identity.
- Document 00.2 establishes vision and mission.
- Document 00.3 establishes scope and boundaries.
- Document 00.4 establishes core principles.

- Document 00.11 establishes security and trust.

This document establishes architectural obligations consistent with all of the above.

Where a conflict exists, the applicable constitutional authority and document-governance rules determine precedence.

## 89. Constitutional Inviolability

The architecture of Veyra must never become an independent source of constitutional authority.

No system, subsystem, service, model, agent, plugin, or external dependency may:

- Redefine Veyra's constitutional identity.
- Override constitutional requirements.
- Grant itself additional authority.
- Circumvent established governance.
- Remove required human oversight.
- Silently weaken security boundaries.
- Treat technical capability as permission.

Architecture must serve the constitution.

The constitution must not be altered merely to accommodate implementation convenience.

## 90. Final Architectural Principle

Veyra must be architected as a coherent and governed system whose components operate within clearly defined responsibilities, interfaces, dependencies, and authority boundaries.

Its architecture must establish a governed relationship between components, data, intelligence, infrastructure, resources, and human authority.

Every significant component must have:

- A defined purpose.
- An identifiable owner.
- An explicit responsibility boundary.
- A governed permission scope.
- Documented dependencies.
- A verifiable relationship with the wider platform.
- A defined lifecycle.

The architecture must support:

- Growth without sacrificing clarity.
- Extensibility without sacrificing control.
- Intelligence without surrendering platform authority.
- Automation without surrendering human oversight.
- Interoperability without unrestricted trust.
- Operational growth without loss of governance.
- Technical progress without compromising constitutional identity.

**The architecture of Veyra must make its principles enforceable, its responsibilities understandable, its boundaries explicit, its operations verifiable, and its evolution governable.**
